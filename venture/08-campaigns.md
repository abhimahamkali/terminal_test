## 1. Your shot-challenge idea: keep the DNA, change the contest

Your instinct — **gather a Gen-Z group, a shot glass, a timer, a countable number, film it** — is
genuinely good, and most of it should survive. But *"how many can you drink in 20 seconds"* has two
problems, and one of them is a safety problem I'd be wrong not to flag.

### Why the chug format is the wrong contest

**1. It's hot liquid.** A speed-drinking contest with hot liquid is a scalding risk — mouth,
throat, oesophagus. Speed-eating and speed-drinking challenges cause real injuries, and a brand
that *organises* one owns that outcome. Add high sodium consumed fast, and you have a format you
cannot safely promote, cannot run with minors, and cannot defend if someone gets hurt on camera.
This alone is disqualifying.

**2. It frames your product as an ordeal.** This is the strategic problem, and it's the bigger one.
A volume-endurance challenge says *"this is a dare"* — which tells every viewer it tastes bad.
You've spent this whole plan establishing **"savoury, not sweet"** as a delicious, aspirational,
zero-sugar choice. A chugging contest says the opposite in five seconds.

The viral mechanic you actually want is **aspiration** — *I'm the kind of person who drinks this* —
not **endurance** — *I survived this*. You said it yourself earlier: you want Gen-Z showing off
that they're on this diet. Nobody shows off surviving something.

**3. A dare buys views, not reorders.** Your entire viability case rests on **repeat purchase**
(`06-viability.md` §6). Stunt content converts to neither trial nor habit.

### What to keep

The shot glass. The group. The timer. The countdown. The countable number. The competition. The
filmed reaction. All of it is good — it just needs to point at something other than volume.

## 2. `THE 20-SECOND BREW` — your idea, repurposed

Keep the **20 seconds**. Move it from *how fast you can drink it* to **how fast it's ready.**

> **Coffee: 4 minutes. Chai: 6 minutes. TAMRA: 20 seconds.**

- **Format:** a hard 20-second countdown on screen. Kettle pours at 0:00. Bag goes in. Colour blooms — beetroot is spectacular here. At 0:20 the timer hits zero and the glass is done. Cut.
- **Why it works:** it's natively short-form, it's the same countdown energy you wanted, it's a *real product benefit* (a café's actual objection is prep time), and it's infinitely repeatable as a content template — three flavours × any setting × any hand.
- **The number becomes your brand property.** "20 seconds" is ownable, repeatable, and true.
- **Zero safety exposure**, and it makes the drink look good instead of making the drinker look brave.

This is the direct home for your idea's energy. Make it your content signature.

## 3. `GUESS WHAT'S IN IT` — the launch campaign

**This is the one I'd lead with.** It uses your Gen-Z group exactly as you imagined, and it's the
single best format for a product whose problem is that nobody knows what it is.

**The setup:** 8–10 Gen-Z people. Nobody told what they're drinking. Clear glass, hot, one sip.
Camera on the face. Then: *"What's in it?"*

**Why it beats every other format:**
- **It weaponises the surprise.** They'll guess soup, broth, tomato, "something Korean", miso. The reveal — *"it's rasam, in a tea bag"* — lands as a **twist**, which is the only way heritage helps you. Rasam as a reveal is delightful; rasam as a headline is a ₹4 price anchor (`07-naming-and-hook.md` §1).
- **It showcases taste, not tolerance.** Genuine first-sip reactions are the most persuasive food content that exists, and you cannot fake them. You only get them once per person — so film it before anyone knows what the brand is.
- **It is free.** Your own friends, one afternoon, a phone. This fits inside the ₹35,000 test.
- **It doubles as research.** You are running blind product validation and shooting your launch campaign in the same session. Score every taster 1–10 and ask *"would you pay ₹90 for this in a café?"* — those answers feed Gate 1 in `06-viability.md` §6 directly.

**Run it the same afternoon as your Gate-1 blind test.** Same room, same kettle, one camera. One
session, two deliverables.

**Outputs from one afternoon:**
| Asset | Use |
|---|---|
| 1 hero reel, 45–60s | Launch post |
| 8–10 single-reaction clips, 8–15s | Six weeks of daily content |
| The "guess" supercut | Highest-shareability cut |
| The reveal clip | Your pinned post |
| Scored tasting sheet | Gate-1 evidence |

## 4. `THE 4PM SWAP` — the campaign that drives repeat purchase

Reactions get attention. This gets **habit**, which is what your P&L needs.

**The ask:** 10 Gen-Z participants swap their 4pm coffee or chai for a TAMRA shot for **7 days**
and post one frame a day.

**Why it's the commercially serious one:**
- **7 days of content per person instead of one.** Ten participants = 70 posts.
- **It builds the habit you're selling.** Someone who does this for a week has a 4pm ritual, and your reorder rate is the metric the whole business turns on.
- **It rides a conversation already running.** Caffeine, cortisol, sleep, afternoon crashes — your audience is already talking about this. You arrive as the answer rather than manufacturing the anxiety.
- **Day 7 is the payoff post:** *"I haven't had a 4pm coffee in a week. I've been sleeping better."*

**Critical legal guardrail:** that sleep line is fine coming from a participant describing their own
week **unpaid and unscripted**. The moment you pay them or script it, it becomes *your* advertising
claim under ASCI and the Consumer Protection Act — and "sleep better" is a health claim you cannot
substantiate. So: **brief every participant in writing** to describe their own experience and never
attribute an effect to the product. Keep the brief on file. Details in `02-compliance-india.md` §2.

## 5. `SAVOURY vs SWEET` — the insight, dramatised

A 15-second recurring format that performs your core line (`07-naming-and-hook.md` §3).

Two glasses at 4pm: a sweet drink (chai, cola, juice, energy drink) and a TAMRA shot. Same person
drinks both. On-screen text: **"Which one do you actually want at 4pm?"**

Cheap, repeatable forever, and every single execution re-teaches your positioning without you
having to explain it. Run it weekly.

## 6. Sequencing and budget

Everything here is free or nearly free, which is the point — you have no marketing budget in the
₹1 lakh plan (`05-first-100k-plan.md` §3).

| Week | Campaign | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | **Guess What's In It** — same session as the Gate-1 blind test | ₹0 (folded into the test) |

**Zero paid spend this quarter.** No boosting, no influencer fees. If the organic reactions don't
move, paid spend won't fix it — it'll just hide the signal you're paying ₹35,000 to get.

## 7. The one metric for all of it

Not views. Not followers. **Saves and shares** — they indicate intent, where views indicate nothing
— and beneath that, the only number that matters:

> **Did a café that listed it reorder?**

A reel with 200,000 views and no reorders means the content worked and the product didn't. Judge
every campaign against the reorder line in `06-viability.md` §6, never against the view count.

## 8. If you still want a competition

You clearly like the competitive format, and there's a version that's safe *and* on-strategy —
because it rewards **discernment** rather than endurance:

> **THE BLIND FLIGHT.** Three unlabelled glasses — Pepper, Lemon, Beet. Identify all three.
> Timed. Leaderboard. Winner gets a month's supply.

It keeps the group, the timer, the competition and the leaderboard. It replaces "who can take the
most punishment" with "who has the best palate" — which flatters the participant, showcases three
SKUs instead of one, and makes the drink look worth paying attention to. And it's genuinely hard,
so the leaderboard means something.

Run it at a café as a Saturday event once you have menu listings. It's a launch party, a content
shoot and a sampling drive in one, and the café will co-promote it because it fills their floor.
