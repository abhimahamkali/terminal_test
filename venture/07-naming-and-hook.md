# 07 — The Hook: What to Call It Instead of Rasam

## 1. The reframe: rasam becomes your recipe, not your product name

You don't need a euphemism for "rasam". You need to move the word **down the pack**, from the
headline to the ingredient story — where heritage adds credibility instead of capping price.

- **In big type:** what it *is* to the drinker (a savoury brew), and what it *doesn't* contain.
- **In small type:** "Made the way rasam has been made for 2,000 years — tamarind, black pepper, cumin, asafoetida."

That small line is an asset. It answers "is this authentic?" and "what's actually in it?" without
ever asking the customer to pay ₹90 for something they file mentally at ₹4 a cup.

**The precedent worth studying: bone broth.** Identical liquid that cooks have simmered for
centuries, sold as stock at near-zero value. One reframe — new vessel, new occasion, new
vocabulary, sold as a *drink* rather than an *ingredient* — and it became a $15–20 wellness
purchase in the US. Nothing about the liquid changed. Only the frame did.

That is precisely the move available to you.

---

## 2. First, pick the price-setting word — this is the trap

Whatever noun you choose silently sets your price ceiling, because customers price by category
before they price by product. In India:

| Noun | Mental price anchor | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **"tea"** | ₹20–60 a cup | ❌ **Avoid.** Feels obvious and comprehensible, and it's format-accurate (it *is* a tea bag) — but it anchors you at a third of your required price. This is the trap. |
| "soup" | ₹40–120, and it's a *meal*, not a drink | ❌ Avoid — puts you at lunch, not at 4pm |
| "broth" | ₹80–150, wellness-coded | ✅ Works |
| **"brew"** | ₹100–250, coffee- and craft-coded | ✅ **Best** |
| "shot" | ₹80–200, espresso/wellness-coded | ✅ Best for the café menu |
| "tonic" / "elixir" | ₹100–250 | ⚠️ Claim-adjacent and dated |

**Recommendation: "brew" for the brand, "shot" for the café menu.** Both borrow coffee's price
permission. Neither has tea's cheapness or soup's mealtime baggage.

---

## 3. The hook, recommended

### Tagline
> # Savoury, not sweet.

Three words. It states the category insight, mirrors the sentence structure of the zero-sugar
marketing you admire ("sugar-free", "no added sugar"), carries no regulated claim, and works as a
bio line, a pack strip, a hashtag and a T-shirt.

*(Why not "Salt, not sugar" — which is tighter and closer to the LMNT playbook? Because Indian
consumers carry real sodium anxiety around hypertension. "Savoury" gets the same contrast with
none of the baggage.)*

### The insight line — the one that makes someone stop scrolling
> **Every drink in India is sweet.**
> Chai. Coffee. Juice. Soda. Lassi. Buttermilk. Energy drinks. Every single one.
> **Here's the first that isn't.**

This is the strongest asset you have, and it's the one nobody has used. It is **true**, it is
**verifiable in three seconds** by anyone who thinks about it, and it makes an invisible gap
visible. It doesn't need you to explain rasam, defend a price, or make a health claim — the
audience does the work themselves. Make this your pinned post and your first slide in every pitch.

### The occasion
> **4 PM.**
> Coffee keeps you up. Chai is just sugar. Water is boring.

Naming an occasion is what turns a product into a habit. 4pm is the one moment when both incumbent
hot drinks genuinely fail — the slump is real, and the caffeine-and-sleep conversation is already
running in your target audience's head. You are not creating that anxiety; you are arriving with
the answer to it.

Secondary occasion: **after dinner** — which is, not coincidentally, when rasam was always served.

### The stat block (front of pack, and the quick-commerce thumbnail)
> ### 0g SUGAR · 0mg CAFFEINE · 0 DAIRY · ~6 KCAL

Every one of these is a factual nutrient-content claim — permitted, free, and requiring no
substantiation dossier. This is your entire legal marketing arsenal and it happens to be exactly
what the category you're borrowing from sells on. Never upgrade it into a health claim
(`02-compliance-india.md` §2).

### The heritage line (small type, back of pack, bottom of the caption)
> Made the way rasam has been made for 2,000 years.
> Tamarind · Black pepper · Cumin · Asafoetida. Nothing else.

"Nothing else" is doing quiet work here — it's a clean-label signal, and it's true.

## 4. The full lockup

```
                    [ BRAND ]
              Savoury Brew · Pepper

            Savoury, not sweet.

   0g SUGAR · 0mg CAFFEINE · 0 DAIRY · ~6 KCAL

   ─────────────────────────────────────────
   Made the way rasam has been made for
   2,000 years. Tamarind, black pepper,
   cumin, asafoetida. Nothing else.

   1 bag · 150ml hot water · 90 seconds
```

**SKU names — no "rasam", no "flavour" language:**

| Was | Now |
|---|---|
| Original | **Pepper** |
| Lemon | **Lemon** |
| Beetroot | **Beet** |

So: *Pepper Brew, Lemon Brew, Beet Brew.* Short, honest, shelf-legible, and they read as varietals
(like a coffee roast) rather than as flavours (like a soft drink).

---

## 5. The café menu line — your highest-leverage copy

This is the single piece of writing that most determines whether this business works, because it's
what a stranger reads at the moment of paying. Print it as:

```
  SAVOURY SHOT                                    ₹90
  Hot, savoury, zero sugar. Pepper · Lemon · Beet
```

Three reasons this specific wording:
- **"Shot"** sets the price and signals small-and-intense, so nobody feels short-changed by 150ml.
- **"Hot, savoury, zero sugar"** pre-answers the only question a customer has — *what will this taste like?* — and flags the one attribute that makes them curious rather than suspicious.
- **No "rasam", no "instant".** A customer who knows rasam will recognise it on the first sip and be delighted. A customer who doesn't is never given a reason to skip it.

## 6. What to say in each channel

| Where | Lead with |
|---|---|
| Instagram bio | *Savoury, not sweet.* |
| First/pinned post | The insight line (§3) |
| Pack front | Brand + "Savoury Brew" + stat block |
| Café menu | *Savoury Shot — hot, savoury, zero sugar* |
| Gym / studio | *Warm savoury. Salt, not sugar.* — the electrolyte frame works here; gym audiences read sodium as performance, not risk. Lead with **Beet** and **Lemon**. |
| Corporate pantry pitch | *The 4pm option that isn't coffee.* Practical, not aspirational. |
| Hotel / chef pitch | *A caffeine-free, zero-sugar hot drink. 20 seconds, no prep, no wastage, carries your logo.* Chefs buy operations, not stories. |
| Quick-commerce thumbnail | The stat block, legible at 200px. Nobody can see a sachet that small. |

## 7. Brand name shortlist

The descriptor above does most of the selling, so the brand name only has to be short, ownable and
sayable. Two directions:

**A. Abstract + hard-working descriptor (recommended — the LMNT route)**
`SIPP` · `TARR` · `NOON` · `OKRA`
→ **NOON — Savoury Brew.** "Noon" quietly carries the time-of-day occasion, reads in English and
travels for export. Short, clean, no heritage baggage.

**B. Occasion as the name**
`4PM` · `AFTER`
→ **4PM** names the problem in two characters, is natively hashtaggable, and makes the occasion
unforgettable. Slight risk: harder to trademark as a bare numeral-word combination, and awkward if
you ever want the morning occasion too.

**Recommendation: `NOON`, with `4PM` as the fallback.** Run the free IP India public search across
**Class 30** (condiments, seasonings, soups) and **Class 32** (preparations for making beverages)
before committing — if you position as a drink, Class 32 is where a competitor could attack you.

---

## 8. One legal note

The **marketing descriptor** ("Savoury Brew") and the **legal food name** are different things and
both appear on pack. FSSAI requires the name of the food and its category — which will be a
standardised head such as *mixed condiment / seasoning* (see `02-compliance-india.md` §2). That
sits in the mandatory declarations block in small type. "Savoury Brew" is the brand descriptor in
large type. This is completely normal and every packaged food does it; just don't let a designer
delete the legal name to make the layout cleaner.

## 9. Words to never put on this pack

| Banned | Why |
|---|---|
| **Rasam** *(in large type)* | Commodity price anchor. Fine in the small-type heritage line. |
| **Instant** | Discount signal. Maggi-adjacent. |
| **Mix / Premix / Powder** | Describes the format, not the benefit. Nobody buys a powder. |
| **Soup** | Puts you at a meal, not at 4pm. |
| **Tea** | Anchors at ₹20–60 (§2). |
| **Digestive / debloat / gut health / appetising** | Regulated health claims. Hard stop. |
| **Natural / pure / fresh** | Separately restricted terms under the labelling regulations. |
| **Traditional recipe from grandmother's kitchen** | True, and it ages your brand out of the Gen-Z audience you're targeting. Let the ingredient list imply heritage; keep the voice modern. |
